# Fathom Mental Models: The Business Judgment Checklist

Reference for judging any business before writing a report (`data/*.json`) or a case
study (`data/case-studies/*.json`). It is the reusable analytical framework distilled
from a full forensic dossier (the Kaspi.kz "Mental Models" method), reorganised into a
checklist that works on any company.

It sits on top of the voice, formatting and honesty rules in the repo `CLAUDE.md` (no
em-dashes, no spaced hyphens, beginner voice, never fabricate) and the
`EDITORIAL-CHECKLIST.md` in this `frameworks/` directory, which still apply in full. This file
is the "what to look for"; those files are the "how to verify it."

The job here is to arrive at a defensible judgment of a business by running it through
every angle, not one. A thesis that survives all of them is strong. A thesis that only
survives one is a guess. Work each section. Do not be polite to the company or to your
own first impression.

---

## How to use this

For a given company, walk the four lenses in order: **Quality**, **Capital**,
**Valuation**, **Risk**. Then run the two outside proofs. Each model below states what
it tests, the one question to ask, and the plain-language version (which is what
eventually reaches the reader in Fathom voice). A company does not need to pass every
model, but you must know where it fails and why.

---

## Lens 1: Business quality

### 1. Toll booth vs restaurant
Separate the irreplaceable asset from the ordinary business bolted onto it. A licence,
a network, a location or a habit that everyone must pass through is the toll booth;
everything built around it is the restaurant. The restaurant can be copied; the toll
booth cannot.
- **Ask:** what is the one thing here a competitor cannot recreate with money and time?
- **Plain version:** some businesses own a gate everyone has to walk through. Others
  just run a shop next to the gate.
- **Red flag:** the "moat" is the shop (service, features, brand polish), not the gate.

### 2. Outsider perception and founder cognition
The people who reframe an industry usually did not grow up inside it. Check whether the
operators inherited the industry's default assumptions or questioned them. Read how
they talk: numbers with no jargon, concrete customer scenarios, "we" not "I", craft not
finance, is the signature of operators who think from first principles.
- **Ask:** what assumption does this company reject that everyone else in the industry
  treats as fixed?
- **Red flag:** management speaks entirely in industry cliches and never in customer
  terms.

### 3. Moat as specific stacked mechanisms
"Moat" is not a label like "super app" or "market leader." It is a specific set of
mechanisms. List each one and rate it on three properties: **depth** (how strong),
**durability** (how long it lasts), **verifiability** (can you see it in disclosed
numbers). Then check whether the layers are **sequential** (each depends on the one
below) and whether they are **multiplicative** (each raises the returns of the others)
rather than merely additive.
- **Ask:** name the mechanisms one by one. Which layer, if broken, collapses the rest?
- **Plain version:** a real moat is a list of specific reasons a customer cannot leave,
  not a single slogan.
- **Red flag:** you can only describe the moat with an adjective, not a mechanism.

### 4. The words vs numbers audit
Take every promise management made and check it against the audited financials,
segment by segment, period by period. The test is whether the talk **describes** the
numbers or **substitutes** for them. Green signals: guidance met (not sandbagged, not
"beaten" by an implausible amount), every headwind named and quantified, metric
definitions held stable across periods, adjustments that reconcile to the headline
arithmetic.
- **Ask:** does every "strong" or "robust" in their commentary come attached to a
  number?
- **Red flag:** metric definitions quietly change when results soften, or adjustments
  do not reconcile.

### 5. Product embeddedness and the displacement test
For a business whose thesis rests on being indispensable, ask what happens to the
customer if it vanished tomorrow. Map embeddedness across daily life, finances, and any
infrastructure it has replaced. The deeper the disruption its disappearance would cause,
the higher the switching cost, and the wider the moat.
- **Ask:** if this product disappeared overnight, what breaks for the customer, and how
  quickly could they replace it?
- **Plain version:** the harder it is to live without, the harder it is to compete with.

## Lens 2: Capital and returns

### 6. Tangible return on equity
High ROE alone is not enough, and acquisitions inflate the equity base with goodwill
that earns nothing. Strip goodwill and intangibles to get return on **tangible** equity.
A drop in headline ROE right after an acquisition is often accounting, not
deterioration.
- **Ask:** is the ROE fall a real decline in the business, or just a larger equity base
  from goodwill?

### 7. Reinvestment runway (the compounding test)
A high return with nowhere to redeploy capital is a dividend stock. A high return that
keeps finding internal reinvestment at similar rates is a compounding machine. Estimate
the incremental return on incremental capital (ROIIC) and confirm there is a real,
multi-year runway to deploy it.
- **Ask:** where does the next retained rupee go, and does it earn near the same return
  as the last one?
- **Red flag:** high historical ROE but marginal capital going into low-return projects.

### 8. Segment economic decomposition
Do not average a mixed business into one number. Break it into segments and ask how
much capital each needs and what each earns. A blended return can hide one
capital-light near-infinite-return engine carrying a capital-heavy ordinary one.
- **Ask:** which segment earns the returns, and how much capital does it actually tie up?

### 9. Cash conversion, segregated by segment
Reported profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. Check whether net income converts to free
cash flow. For businesses with a lending arm, a growing loan book shows up as cash
outflow and understates the platform's true conversion, so segregate the
cash-generating business from the cash-absorbing financing arm.
- **Ask:** is weak cash conversion a quality problem, or just a growing loan book
  absorbing cash it will earn back later?

### 10. Negative working capital and float
Some businesses are funded by their own customers and suppliers: the customer pays
before the business pays its suppliers. That float is free leverage that never appears
in the debt line. It boosts returns without adding risk.
- **Ask:** who is funding the working capital here, the company or its customers?

### 11. Leverage quality
Not all debt is equal. Good leverage is cheap, sticky and self-renewing (customer
deposits, interest-free payables, long-dated fixed-rate debt). Bad leverage is
short-term, variable-rate, currency-mismatched, or covenant-heavy. A business funded by
sticky deposits is structurally different from one funded by commercial paper, even at
the same leverage ratio.
- **Ask:** if funding costs spiked or credit froze, would this liability run, or stay?

### 12. Lifetime value vs acquisition cost
For a business with sticky customers, the real value is the present value of future
earnings from the existing base. Compare lifetime value per customer against the cost to
acquire one. Very high ratios mean each existing customer is a multi-year annuity, which
is exactly what a simple price-to-earnings multiple misses.
- **Ask:** what is one customer worth over their life, versus what it cost to win them?

### 13. Capital allocation under pressure (the Munger test)
The real test of management is what they do with cash when the obvious move is wrong.
Good allocators wait for opportunities, buy back stock when it is cheap (not at peaks),
return cash when reinvestment is weak, and walk away from bad deals. Bad allocators buy
at high multiples, chase growth, and hoard or splurge at the wrong times.
- **Ask:** across the full history, did capital go to the highest-return use available
  at each moment, or to empire-building?
- **Watch:** the first large acquisition into an unproven geography is where discipline
  gets tested. Escalating commitment to justify an earlier decision is the failure mode.

## Lens 3: Valuation

### 14. Existing-book value as a floor
Ignore all growth. Value only the existing customer base or asset base as if nothing
ever changes. This gives a floor on intrinsic value. If that floor is already near the
market price, the market is paying nothing for growth, optionality, or new markets, and
everything else is free.
- **Ask:** what is the business worth assuming zero growth from here?

### 15. Normalized earning power
Strip out cyclical and one-time headwinds (a rate spike, a one-off tax, a temporary
supply shock, losses from a deliberate investment) to find what the machine actually
earns in a normal year. Reported earnings in a bad year can badly understate structural
earning power.
- **Ask:** what would this earn with the temporary drags removed, and are those drags
  really temporary?

### 16. Reverse DCF (back out the implied belief)
Instead of estimating value and comparing to price, take the price as given and solve
for the growth rate the market must be assuming. Then compare that implied rate to the
company's demonstrated underlying growth. A wide gap is the mispricing, in either
direction.
- **Ask:** what growth is the current price implying, and does the evidence support it?
- **Plain version:** the price is a bet. Work out what the market is betting on, then
  check if it is likely.

### 17. Acquisition price audit
When management buys something, audit the price the way you would audit any purchase.
Compare the multiple paid against comparable transactions, and against the cost of
building the same thing from scratch (greenfield cost plus years of execution risk).
Paying the greenfield cost for a finished, turnkey asset can be a good deal even at a
high headline multiple.
- **Ask:** did they pay a fair price versus both the comps and the build-it-yourself
  cost, and is the downside bounded?

## Lens 4: Risk

### 18. Endogenous vs exogenous inversion
Sort every risk into risks inside the business (competition, execution, a bad product, a
bad acquisition) and risks outside it (the country, geopolitics, regulation, the macro).
Most companies carry mainly internal risk you can analyse your way through. A rare few
have near-zero internal risk and concentrated external risk. For those, you are not
betting on the company, you are betting on its environment. Say so plainly.
- **Ask:** are the real thesis-breakers inside this business or outside it?

### 19. Positioning vs exposure
For each risk, separate whether the company is merely exposed or is actively positioning
against it. Management can neutralise most controllable risks (through product design,
diversification, compliance). What it cannot position against is what is left. The
quality of that positioning, not the existence of the risk, is what matters.
- **Ask:** which risks has management hedged, and which are simply irreducible?

### 20. Legible vs structural risk
Markets over-weight the loud, dramatic, easily-told risk (a short-seller report, a
scary headline, a visible competitor) and under-weight the quiet structural one (a
succession gap, a slow regulatory shift). The analytical edge is usually worrying about
the quiet one and ignoring the loud one.
- **Ask:** what is the market most afraid of here, and is that actually the thing that
  could break the thesis?

## The two outside proofs

### 21. The counterfactual test
The strongest evidence that a moat is structural rather than lucky is a bigger,
better-funded competitor that tried the same thing and lost. If the incumbent giant
copied every feature and still could not win, the moat is real, and its remaining hope
(often, that a regulator changes the rules) tells you it could not win on the merits.
- **Ask:** has a larger rival attempted this and failed, and if so, why exactly?

### 22. Insider behaviour over the ownership snapshot
A static ownership percentage says little. The behaviour is in the flows. Founders
selling into an IPO to create a float is different from founders dumping stock. Founders
buying with their own money at a low point, transferring shares to each other (real
trust), and taking tiny salaries with wealth in equity, all signal alignment. The
absence of pledged or margined shares is a further positive.
- **Ask:** what have the insiders actually done with their shares over time, and at what
  prices?

### 23. Sophisticated-buyer validation
When the single most credible operator or investor in a category puts real capital into
a business, that is an expert endorsement of the model. Note the limit: an equity stake
validates the business; only a disclosed commercial agreement proves an operating
partnership. Credit the validation, not the imagined partnership.
- **Ask:** who with the most information about this model is buying, and does the price
  they paid confirm the mispricing?

## The synthesis

Close on three verdicts and then the only question that matters.

- **Business verdict.** Which lens-1 and lens-2 tests did it pass, and where is the one
  honest blemish?
- **Risk verdict.** Endogenous or exogenous? What are the two or three genuine
  thesis-breakers, and how are they positioned against?
- **Valuation verdict.** Where does the price sit against the existing-book floor, the
  normalized earning power, and the reverse-DCF implied belief?
- **The question:** at this price, given all three, what has to go right, and how long is
  that list? The longer the list, the worse the deal, however good the business.

The recurring discipline underneath every model above: separate the **legible and
cyclical** from the **structural**. Headline profit from underlying profit. Standard ROE
from tangible ROE. Reported earnings from normalized earning power. The loud risk from
the real one. The edge is almost always in that separation.
