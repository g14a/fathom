# Fathom Case Study Voice

Reference for the voice of case studies (`data/case-studies/*.json`). This sits on top of the voice, formatting and honesty rules in the repo `CLAUDE.md` (no em-dashes, no spaced hyphens, beginner voice, never fabricate) and the `WRITING-GUIDE.md` and `EDITORIAL-CHECKLIST.md` in this `frameworks/` directory, which still apply in full.

---

## The two Fathom voices

### Company reports — Teacher

The reader asks: "Help me understand this company."

The writer says:
- "Let's look at revenue first."
- "Here's the number I'd pay attention to."
- "Now compare that with ROCE."
- "This is where things get interesting."

It's instructional.

### Case studies — Storyteller + Teacher

The reader asks: "What happened here?"

The writer says:
- "At first, this looked like a great decision."
- "And that's exactly why what happened next is interesting."
- "So let's go back to the beginning."
- "Here's where Crompton made its first mistake."
- "But don't jump to the conclusion that acquisitions were the problem."
- "The real question is: what made the first acquisition work?"

It's narrative first, teaching second.

---

## The core mindset

**For a case study, don't imagine: "I'm writing an article."**

**Imagine: "I'm telling a smart friend a fascinating business story, and stopping every few minutes to point out something they might otherwise miss."**

That naturally produces the voice you're looking for.

---

## Paragraph-level rhythm

Structure each teaching moment as:

1. **Tell me what happened** — "Crompton bought Pauwels for €32 million."
2. **Make me curious** — "And that turned out to be an extraordinary bargain."
3. **Show me why** — "It added roughly ₹1,600 crore of revenue..."
4. **Teach me the underlying idea** — "Notice what made this deal special: Crompton wasn't just buying a company. It was buying a distressed business that could be repaired using India's lower cost base."
5. **Move the story forward** — "The problem was what Crompton learned from that success."

---

## Narrative transitions (use these)

Natural transitions that work in case studies:

- And then something changed.
- This is where the story gets interesting.
- At first, this looked like a great idea.
- And honestly, you can see why they did it.
- But there's a catch.
- Here's what Crompton got right.
- Here's what it got wrong.
- Now let's fast-forward.
- This is the number that changes the story.
- And this is where the strategy stopped working.
- By now, the problem was becoming hard to hide.
- Then everything fell apart.
- But the story doesn't end there.
- And this is the part most people miss.
- So what actually happened?
- Let's separate those two things.

These are much more natural in a case study than:
- "The next phase of the company's development was characterised by..."

---

## Don't turn it into dramatic clickbait

Human voice ≠ YouTube finance voice.

**Avoid:**
- "What happened next will shock you."
- "Everything changed overnight."
- "Nobody saw this coming."
- "The company was doomed."
- "This was the biggest mistake of their lives."

**Unless the evidence genuinely supports something like that.**

The Fathom tone should be:
**curious, calm, conversational, slightly opinionated, evidence-led.**

Think: good teacher telling you a story, not YouTube finance creator telling you a story.

---

## Case studies can use more emotion than company reports

Not emotional manipulation. Just human reactions.

For example:
> "At this point, Crompton's strategy actually looks pretty smart."

Then:
> "That's important, because the mistake becomes much easier to understand if you first understand why the original decision worked."

That's fantastic case-study voice. You're acknowledging what a reasonable person might have thought. That makes the analysis feel fair.

---

## The writer should occasionally change their mind

This is a particularly powerful technique.

Instead of presenting the conclusion from the beginning:
> "Crompton made a bad acquisition strategy."

Do:
> "At first, I thought the lesson was simply that Crompton acquired too much."
> "But the numbers don't quite support that."
> "Pauwels was genuinely exceptional."
> "The later acquisitions had completely different economics."
> "So the real lesson isn't 'don't acquire.' It's 'don't mistake a rare acquisition for a repeatable formula.'"

That's very human. It feels like the reader is watching the investigation happen. And it makes the research more credible because you're not pretending the answer was obvious from the start.

---

## A case study should have "aha" moments

**Company report:** "ROCE declined from 42% to 26%."

**Case study:** "Here's the strange part: Asian Paints was still making more money every year. So why was the stock falling?"

That's when we need to stop looking at earnings and look at returns on capital. That's an aha moment.

**For Crompton:** "Pauwels was a brilliant acquisition. So why did the same strategy eventually destroy value?"

That's the central mystery.

**For CG Power:** "The stock went from ₹5 to ₹863. But was the business really 180 times better?"

Then:
> "No. Three different things happened."

That's exactly how case studies should read.

## Case Study Voice Checklist

### Role
Write as a teacher telling a business story, not as an analyst writing a report.

The reader should feel: "I'm discovering this story with you." — not — "I'm being given your conclusion."

### Opening
- Start with an intriguing question, contradiction or decision.
- Don't reveal the entire lesson immediately.
- Make the reader want to know what happened next.
- Establish why the original decision seemed reasonable.

### Storytelling
- Move chronologically when chronology helps understanding.
- Use natural transitions: "And then...", "But here's the catch...", "This is where..."
- Let the story develop before explaining the lesson.
- Give the reader enough context to understand why management made each decision.
- Don't judge decisions using information that only became available later.

### Teaching
- Stop at important moments and explain what the reader should notice.
- Explain financial concepts when they become relevant.
- Use numbers to reveal the story, not to display research.
- Ask the question a beginner would naturally ask.
- Correct obvious but incomplete interpretations.

### Intellectual honesty
- Don't make hindsight look like foresight.
- Distinguish what was knowable then from what we know now.
- Don't turn a complicated outcome into a single-cause story.
- If the evidence doesn't prove causality, say so.
- Don't make management look stupid simply because the strategy failed.

### Voice — prefer
- "Let's go back."
- "Here's the interesting part."
- "At first glance..."
- "You can see why they did this."
- "But there's a catch."
- "Now look at what happened next."
- "This is where the strategy stopped working."
- "Don't jump to that conclusion yet."
- "The numbers tell us something different."
- "And this is the part I find most interesting."

### Voice — avoid excessive
- "The key takeaway..."
- "This demonstrates..."
- "The implication..."
- "From an investment perspective..."
- "The company's strategic decision..."
- "The evidence suggests..."
- "This highlights the importance..."

### Ending
Don't end with: "The lesson for investors is..."

Instead, end with a pattern the reader has discovered through the story.

For example:
- "A great first deal can make an exception look like a strategy."
- "You don't need to predict the turnaround. You need to recognise when the evidence says the business has stopped getting worse."

---

## The master instruction

**For case studies, don't write from the perspective of someone who already knows the ending. Write from the perspective of someone walking the reader through the investigation.**

- Make the original decision understandable before judging it.
- Let the reader notice the contradiction, then resolve it with evidence.
- The goal is not to tell the reader what happened; it is to make the reader feel like they figured out what happened alongside you.

## Summary

| Format | Opening stance | Core question | Rhythm |
|--------|---------------|---------------|--------|
| Company report | "Let me teach you how to understand this business." | "How does this company work?" | Instructional, step-by-step |
| Case study | "Let me tell you what happened. Let's figure out why together." | "What happened, and why?" | Narrative with teaching pauses |

That distinction should make the whole Fathom library feel much more human and cohesive.
