# Fathom Anti-AI-Slop Rule

Applies to everything with prose: signals, case studies, company reports, sector
explainers. It sits on top of the voice, formatting and honesty rules in the repo
`CLAUDE.md` and the per-format guides, which still apply in full.

Fathom should sound like a smart teacher explaining something over coffee, not like
a writer trying to produce quotable sentences. The failure mode is not stupidity.
It is writing that is too perfectly built: every paragraph carrying a thesis, a
contrast, a punchline and a takeaway. Humans do not write that way. Good teaching
has boring connective tissue between the clever bits.

## The goal

Make the intelligence feel effortless. You are not trying to sound less smart. You
are trying to sound less engineered. Paradoxically, writing that is slightly less
perfectly written reads as more human.

## What to avoid (or ration hard)

- **Manufactured three-part structures.** A real framework can be three parts (the
  currency signal's Earn / Spend / Owe is deliberate and stays). Do not then wrap
  every nearby idea in its own three: "three things shrink the benefit", "three
  buckets", "three questions", "three clean boxes". One intentional trio per piece.
- **Repeated rhetorical opposites.** "Helps if X, hurts if Y" and "dollars in / out"
  land once. After the first two occurrences the reader has it. Cut the rest.
- **Density of quotable lines.** A genuinely memorable sentence every few paragraphs
  starts to feel machine-made. Keep the strongest one or two per section and let
  the other paragraphs simply explain.
- **Formulaic teacher transitions.** "This is where...", "Here's the...", "The real
  question is...", "The important thing is...", "This teaches us...", "But do not
  stop there...", and a run of "But..." openers. Individually fine; together they
  create an obvious instructional cadence. Reduce them by about a quarter.
- **Editorial-framework language.** Sentences that describe the article's own
  structure ("this signal teaches the general rule that failure is one instance
  of...") instead of just teaching the thing.
- **Perfectly symmetrical sentences** that exist mainly to be clever.

## Prefer the plainer version

- Prefer "Let's look at Infosys." over "This is where the theory meets reality."
- Prefer "Real companies don't fit neatly into these boxes." over "The three cases
  are teaching tools, not filing cabinets."
- Prefer "The business grew 4.2%. The rupee made that look like 6.1%." over "The
  difference is the rupee doing work the business did not."
- Prefer "Now consider an airline." over "Now flip the situation."
- Prefer naming the concrete next thing ("The balance sheet creates a different
  problem.") over dramatising it ("Now the dangerous version, the one beginners
  never see coming...").

Allow some paragraphs to just explain, connect two ideas, or move the story along.
"Okay, so that's the basic idea. Now let's look at the numbers." is boring, and
that is good. It is the connective tissue that makes the clever lines land.

## The test before shipping

Read the piece and count: three-part structures, rhetorical opposites, and lines
that were obviously built to be quotable. If any of the three is showing up every
few paragraphs, thin it out. The core idea should survive, stated once, clearly,
with less scaffolding around it.
