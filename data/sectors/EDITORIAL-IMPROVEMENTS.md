# Fathom Editorial Improvements

A working checklist for writing and refining sector pages (and, where relevant,
case studies). It sits on top of `WRITING-GUIDE.md` and the repo `CLAUDE.md`
voice and formatting rules (no em-dashes, no spaced hyphens, beginner voice,
honesty rules), which still apply in full.

## The one principle above all

Whenever introducing a new concept, ask: **"Can the reader apply this idea to a
completely different business tomorrow?"** If the answer is no, rewrite the
explanation until the concept becomes transferable rather than specific. This is
what makes Fathom a course in business thinking, not a library of sector guides.

## Editorial checklist

**1. Every sector should teach one unforgettable idea.**
At the end of writing, ask: "If the reader remembers only one sentence five years
later, what should it be?" Rewrite until everything supports that one idea.

**2. Strengthen the opening mental model.**
The first paragraph should reduce the whole industry to one memorable sentence.
A bank is a spread machine. Software is built once and billed forever. Insurance
earns money twice. A hotel sells perishable inventory. Cement is a regional
commodity. Steel is a price-taker. FMCG wins on shelves, not recipes. The reader
should understand the industry within 30 seconds.

**3. Make the six questions visually scannable.**
For every question: start with a one-sentence answer, then explain, then end with
one concrete example. A reader should skim the six questions and understand the
whole industry.

**4. Explain intuition before terminology.**
Never introduce a metric before explaining why it exists. Instead of leading with
EV, first explain why PE fails, then introduce EV. Do this for every
industry-specific metric.

**5. Add one visual analogy per major concept (one, not five).**
Interest is rent on money. Spectrum is lanes on a highway. Insurance is a
community pot. Hotel rooms are airline seats. Distribution is shelf space. Float
is money waiting for its job.

**6. Strengthen the beginner question.**
It should be the exact question someone naturally asks after reading the opening,
and remove the single biggest mental block before continuing.

**7. Make the "One sentence to remember" exceptional.**
Do not summarize the article. Extract the permanent principle. It should teach a
reusable way of thinking.

**8. Explain why the metric exists, don't define it.**
Explain why investors invented it. Why EV exists. Why RevPAR exists. Why NRR
matters. Why EBITDA/tonne exists. Why profit/vehicle matters. Readers remember
reasons better than definitions.

**9. Constantly compare industries.**
"Unlike software...", "Unlike FMCG...", "Like airlines...", "Similar to banks..."
Comparisons help readers build mental models instead of isolated facts.

**10. Introduce one surprising insight per sector.**
One idea that makes the reader stop. Insurance is really an investment company in
disguise. For banks, deposits are the entire business. In FMCG, the moat is the
shelf, not the formula. Empty hotel rooms are permanently lost inventory. For
steel, China matters more than management. Readers remember surprises.

**11. Reduce unnecessary repetition.**
The same idea should not be repeated across the opening, the six questions, the
main section, and the conclusion. Mention it once, then build on it.

**12. Every section should answer "So what?"**
After every paragraph ask: why should an investor care? If it doesn't answer
that, remove or rewrite it.

**13. Strengthen transitions.**
Connect ideas instead of switching topics. Not "Here's valuation..." but "Once
you understand how this business earns money, the next question is how investors
value those future earnings." Transitions should feel inevitable.

**14. Make examples more concrete.**
Prefer specifics. Not "a company raises prices" but "a ₹10 biscuit becomes ₹11."
Not "volume increases" but "a factory running at 60% utilisation moves to 80%."
Concrete numbers improve retention.

**15. Build reusable investor habits.**
Teach one question the reader can ask about every company in the sector. Banks:
where are deposits coming from? Insurance: is this company better at pricing risk
or investing float? SaaS: are existing customers spending more? Telecom: is ARPU
improving? Hotels: is RevPAR rising from occupancy or from pricing? Steel: is this
actually the low-cost producer?

**16. End with action, not summary.**
Leave the reader with a framework they can apply to the next company they study,
thinking "Now I know exactly what to look for."

## Site-wide additions (built)

These render in the "Take these ideas further" block at the end of each sector
page (`mentalModels`, `relatedSectors`, `relatedCaseStudies` on the `Sector`
type).

**17. Related Mental Models.** Transferable concepts each sector teaches, shown as
chips (Pricing Power, Operating Leverage, Network Effects, Capital Intensity,
Switching Costs, Brand, Distribution, Capital Allocation, Cost Leadership,
Cyclicality, Float, and so on).

**18. Related Sectors.** Links to 2-3 sectors with related economics (Banks ↔
NBFCs ↔ Insurance; Cement ↔ Metals ↔ Infrastructure; Hotels ↔ Hospitals ↔
Airlines).

**19. Related Case Studies.** Where a case study demonstrates the sector's
principles, it is linked (Telecom → Airtel, Capital Markets → BSE, Power →
Suzlon, Auto → Mahindra). Add more as new case studies are written.

## The consistent philosophy every page must answer

1. How does this business make money?
2. Why does it keep making money?
3. Where does the money disappear?
4. What usually destroys it?
5. Why can't competitors copy it?
6. How should investors think about valuing it?

If a section does not reinforce one of these, reconsider whether it belongs.
